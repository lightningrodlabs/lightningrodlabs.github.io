    WebRTC without a signaling server

    Eric Harris-Braun · September 22, 2026

    Two libraries have come out of the work on Presence, our video-call tool for Moss, and I want to write about both. This post is about the first, @lightningrodlabs/webrtc-peer: give it two callbacks, one that sends a message to a peer and one you call when a message arrives, and it hands you back a managed WebRTC connection per peer. It establishes the connection, keeps it alive, and tells you one coherent thing about what it's doing. The next post is about what we do when WebRTC can't connect at all.

    The server in the middle of every WebRTC tutorial

    WebRTC is peer-to-peer once it's connected. Getting it connected is not. Two browsers have to exchange an offer, an answer, and a stream of ICE candidates before a single packet of media flows, and WebRTC deliberately says nothing about how those messages travel. So every tutorial opens by standing up a signaling server, usually a WebSocket relay, and every deployment carries it forever after.

    In a Holochain app that server just isn't needed. Agents can already send each other messages directly, through what Holochain calls remote signals, and the networking layer underneath handles NAT traversal and relaying because it has to for everything else. Presence has done its WebRTC signaling this way from the start. There is no server; the offer goes to the other agent the same way a chat message would.

    That's the good news, and it's real. The less good news is what you're left with once the signaling problem disappears: a raw RTCPeerConnection, which gives you primitives, not a connection. You still have to handle both sides negotiating at once, which corrupts signaling state if you let it. You have to decide when a dropped ICE path deserves a quick ICE restart and when it needs a full teardown, and how long to wait between tries. You have to collapse four independent state machines, ICE, DTLS, signaling and the data channel, into one answer your UI can show. And when a connection fails on someone's laptop in another country, you need a structured record of what happened, not console logs.

    Two years with simple-peer

    Presence started in December 2023, built by Matthias on simple-peer, the library most people reach for. It served us for two years, and I don't want to be unkind to it. But by early 2026 we were maintaining a fork, and the field logs kept describing the same shape of failure in different clothes.

    The one that finally moved us was a reconnection loop we captured in March. A peer connecting through a VPN would connect fine the first time. Then something ordinary would happen, a video toggle or an ICE timeout, the connection would drop, and every reconnection attempt after that would fail: ICE checking for fifteen seconds, disconnected, retry a quarter of a second later, forever, until the user left the room. Leaving and waiting was the only cure, and the timing told the story: come back after ten seconds and it failed again, come back after fifty and it worked. Our retries were hammering a NAT that wanted to be left alone, and the harder we retried the longer it sulked. Retrying is not recovering.

    At the end of March I rewrote the connection layer as a proper state machine. The W3C "perfect negotiation" pattern for glare, so both sides offering at once resolves instead of corrupting. A serialized signaling queue so SDP operations can't interleave. One lifecycle, idle through signaling, connecting, connected, reconnecting, disconnected, failed and closed, with guarded transitions, so an illegal move is logged rather than silently taken. A two-tier reconnection policy that tries a cheap ICE restart first and a full reconnect only after that, with backoff. And a transition log that captures a snapshot of every transport state on every move, because the thing we had been missing most was evidence.

    For a few months the two carriers ran side by side in Presence with per-peer selection, which let us compare them in real rooms. simple-peer was deleted from the codebase at the end of July.

    Pulling it out

    The connection code turned out to know nothing about Holochain beyond a small adapter that turned "send this to that agent" into a remote signal. So on May 20 it became a package. What it asks of your transport is deliberately little: a stable, authenticated identity per peer, since the library assigns polite and impolite negotiation roles by comparing peer ids and trusts you about who is talking; agent-to-agent delivery, no broadcast; and best-effort delivery. Loss, reordering and duplicates are all tolerated, and a connection-scoped session id filters out signals left over from a previous attempt. You do not need a reliable ordered channel, a relay, or a rendezvous service. Anything that can ship an opaque JSON blob from one peer to another will do: Holochain remote signals, a libp2p stream, a plain WebSocket.

    It's layered, so you take only the tier you need. The core is a thin perfect-negotiation wrapper around RTCPeerConnection. On top of that sits the single-peer state machine with the reconnection policy. On top of that, a ConnectionManager that owns every peer in a room, routes inbound signals to the right one, propagates your local media, and exposes a view model per peer with the phase, whether the path is relayed, whether tracks are flowing, and a retry countdown. No runtime dependencies, and the browser's RTCPeerConnection is injectable, so the whole thing runs headless under a mock in a few seconds. Presence also runs it nightly against real connections in a browser harness, establishing, silently dropping and handing over, because a mock can only fail the ways you taught it to.

    What the field kept teaching

    Extraction was not the end of the learning. Every version since has been driven by a log file from a real call, and a few of the changes are worth telling because they generalize.

    Connected should mean media is flowing. Early on, "connected" meant ICE up, DTLS up, and the data channel open. In the field we saw calls held in "connecting" with audio and video flowing perfectly, because the data channel was stuck. Media rides on ICE plus DTLS; the data channel is a separate thing that can be recovered in place in the background. So since 0.3.0 the phase means media-ready, and data-channel readiness is its own flag.

    Emit state changes, not log entries. The manager used to raise its state-changed event from the transition log, so sub-phase entries during a live call, an ICE blip, a data-channel recreate, surfaced as "connected to connected" changes. The app dutifully re-ran its on-connect work each time, re-adding tracks and triggering renegotiation on a call that was fine. That's a bug pattern I now watch for everywhere: an event stream and a log stream are different things.

    Both sides need a shared idea of which attempt is current. In June we chased a reconnect deadlock of about twenty seconds. Each side's connection minted its own random id, and after a teardown a fresh state machine on one side could latch onto the dead session's id and reject the live peer's answers as stale. The fix was an orchestrator-allocated epoch on every signal that survives recreation and is authoritative for ordering. In August the same theme came back at a finer grain and got scoped to the remote side's connection id, with tombstones so a resurrected dead session can't sneak back in.

    Back off, and stop shouting. The August field session that produced 0.5.0 had a dead relay drive eleven back-to-back retry sessions in under two and a half minutes, each one re-flooding some thirty-five ICE candidates at the peer. The retry gap is now exponential, from a few hundred milliseconds out to seven seconds, and outbound candidates are filtered and deduplicated. The same session exposed that giving up from the disconnected state was an illegal transition in our own table, so the machine logged it as blocked and sat there dead instead of failing cleanly. The lifecycle guard did its job by telling us; we had just never read that particular line before.

    And one that's about us, not WebRTC. After 0.5.0 we discovered that Presence had shipped two releases still bundling 0.4.0, because the app's dependency range said ^0.4.0 and npm had quietly pulled the older version from the registry instead of linking the workspace copy. We found it by reading a field log and seeing a "blocked" transition that could not happen under 0.5.0. There's now a test that walks the app's module resolution and fails if it lands anywhere but the workspace package. Fixes that don't ship aren't fixes.

    What it doesn't do

    It does not do discovery, identity, authentication or transport; those are your peer-to-peer substrate's job, and in our case Holochain's. The selective-forwarding roles in the type definitions are reserved markers for a planned SFU mode, not working code; only mesh works today, and the roadmap says what SFU would take. And it cannot conjure a path where there isn't one. When two peers can't hole-punch to each other and nobody is running a TURN server, the state machine will tell you very precisely that it failed, and that's all it can do.

    That last case is where the second library comes in. The bytes for the offer got through, after all. Next post.
